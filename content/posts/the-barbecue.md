---
title: "The Barbecue"
date: "2026-11-10"
summary: "I gave my highest evaluation to a commander whose own company threw a party when he left. It taught me to build more than one path to the truth."
---

**I learned my most important leadership lesson at a company barbecue.**

I assumed command of an airborne infantry battalion on a Friday.

The following Tuesday, a 7.2 magnitude earthquake devastated Haiti.

By the next Friday, we were on the ground in Port-au-Prince.

I didn't have time to build relationships or truly understand my new organization. We shifted immediately from preparing for airborne assaults and combat operations to humanitarian relief: clearing rubble, supporting hospitals, evacuating casualties by helicopter, distributing food and water, and helping thousands of displaced people survive.

Like every new commander, I had to make judgments with incomplete information.

I relied heavily on my company commanders. I issued mission orders with broad intent and trusted them to adapt to rapidly changing conditions on the ground.

One commander quickly distinguished himself. He accepted the hardest missions, required little guidance, and consistently delivered results.

I decided he was one of my strongest leaders.

When we returned to Fort Bragg, we began our summer change of command cycle switching out almost our entire team. Before his ceremony, I counseled the officer I believed was my best company commander and gave him my highest evaluation. As a new battalion commander, I had very few "top block" evaluations to give.

I believed he had earned one.

The following day, after his change of command ceremony, I wandered into a company barbecue.

I assumed they were celebrating the incoming commander.

They weren't.

They were celebrating the outgoing commander leaving.

That afternoon changed the way I led.

Over the next several weeks, I discovered that mission success had hidden the real story. I had mistaken mission accomplishment for leadership effectiveness.

The failure wasn't his.

It was mine.

I realized I had collected almost all of my information through a single channel. I hadn't built enough independent ways to learn the truth.

That realization permanently changed how I approached leadership.

Years later, I read a memorandum by COL Brian Pentecost, a former battalion commander in the 75th Ranger Regiment and a member of the Ranger Hall of Fame, describing the importance of multiple lines of communication. His paper gave language to something I had already learned the hard way: senior leaders must deliberately build multiple, independent paths to the truth.

From that point forward, I deliberately built those paths.

I sought information through informal conversations, peers, subordinate leaders, senior NCOs, sensing sessions, anonymous surveys, retention trends, promotion rates, command climate assessments, and every other source that helped me understand what was really happening inside the organization.

No single report, briefing, or individual ever determined my judgment.

Together, they painted a far more accurate picture than any one source ever could.

I realized this wasn't just about evaluating leaders.

It became the way I approached every important decision.

Every promotion places another layer between leaders and reality.

If we don't deliberately build independent lines of communication, we eventually begin seeing the organization through someone else's eyes.

That lesson changed far more than how I evaluated leaders.

It changed how I built information systems.

Every assessment, survey, sensing session, peer comparison, and performance measure I used afterward answered one question:

**How do I know this is true?**
