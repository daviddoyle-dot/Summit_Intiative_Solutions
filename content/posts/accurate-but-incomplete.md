---
title: "Accurate but Incomplete"
date: "2026-11-17"
summary: "The dashboard told me our refueling sites were slipping. It couldn't tell me why. Going to see for myself changed the diagnosis."
---

The farther leaders move from the work, the more they depend on someone else's interpretation of reality.

That's not a criticism.

It's the nature of leadership.

Dashboards, reports, metrics, trend analysis, and briefings are essential. As organizations grow, no executive can personally observe every process, every customer, every employee, or every problem.

I believe deeply in data. I've been called a "data nerd," and I don't mind.

But every one of those data tools is also a filter.

While commanding the 4th Infantry Division, we planned and executed several long-distance aviation operations. Our Forward Arming and Refueling Points (FARPs) were meeting training requirements on paper, yet timelines during execution continued to slip.

![Soldiers refueling an AH-64 Apache helicopter](/uploads/blog/2026/apache-refuel-1600x900.jpg)

The dashboard told me something was wrong.

It couldn't tell me why.

So I went to several of our FARPs in the field.

With the Brigade Commander and Battalion Commanders, I spent time with the 92F fuelers who were building the site and refueling AH-64 Apaches. I learned how they connected the D-1 nozzle, managed the emergency breakaway system, established the site, and worked under the pressure of rotating aircraft. I even pushed JP8 into an Apache that was on mission.

I wasn't there because I doubted my leaders.

I wasn't there to inspire the Soldiers.

I was there because I didn't yet understand the problem well enough to help solve it.

What I saw changed my understanding of the system.

The issue wasn't effort or commitment.

It wasn't a lack of desire to train.

The problem was the training architecture itself.

That observation led us to bring in additional expertise from Fort Rucker and the National Training Center. Together, we changed how the unit trained and supported one of the most technically demanding parts of aviation operations.

The reports weren't wrong.

They were accurate but incomplete.

That's an important distinction.

Data identified where the friction existed.

Observation helped explain why.

Experience helped determine what to do next.

I've become convinced that executive leaders shouldn't use firsthand observation to replace data.

They should use data to decide where firsthand observation will have the greatest value.

Every promotion places another layer between leaders and reality.

The best leaders don't ignore dashboards.

They use dashboards to decide where they need to see reality for themselves.
