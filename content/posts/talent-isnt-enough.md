---
title: "Talent Isn't Enough"
date: "2026-12-29"
summary: "From Haditha Dam to Haiti to an $88 million storm rebuild, the teams that succeeded believed they would."
---

Many leaders believe that if they surround themselves with the right talent, they will be able to achieve any goal or objective.

Talent matters.

But talent alone is rarely enough.

If talented people are going to succeed, they must be well-led, well-prepared, and most importantly they must believe they are going to accomplish the mission despite any challenge.

Leaders do not create that belief through slogans or motivational speeches. They build it through trust, preparation, repetition, and shared hardship.

The seizure of the Haditha Dam during the Iraq War, humanitarian response efforts in Port-au-Prince after the Haiti earthquake, and rebuilding a Combat Aviation Brigade’s helicopters after catastrophic storm damage may seem like completely unrelated events.

But they all had one thing in common:

The people involved believed they were going to succeed despite apparently insurmountable obstacles. They had been conditioned to accomplish their missions.

As my good friend and fellow Ranger, Tony Mayne, pointed out, a group of Army Rangers left their base on 30 March 2003 to accomplish one difficult mission. On 31 March, they received another totally different and remarkably demanding mission. On 1 April, they seized a 13-story, two-mile-wide piece of critical infrastructure using only a five-paragraph operations order derived from a Ranger School handbook.

They succeeded because they trusted their training, trusted each other, and believed they could accomplish the mission.

In 2010, an Airborne Infantry Battalion from the 82d Airborne Division had been preparing for combat operations in Afghanistan. Within a week of the earthquake in Haiti, they ceased that training and were conducting humanitarian assistance and disaster relief in Port-au-Prince.

They succeeded because they adapted quickly, trusted their leaders, and believed they could help bring order to an overwhelming situation.

Years later, in 2024, after two unexpected Colorado microbursts damaged \$88 million worth of aircraft in a Combat Aviation Brigade, the unit had to shift immediately from preparing for a self-deployment mission to Louisiana to rebuilding its fleet.

The Brigade used predictive analysis tools, many of which they developed themselves, to organize the problem, the people, the parts, and the plan. They believed they could rebuild in time to meet standards and deploy overseas on schedule—and they worked relentlessly to make it happen.

When leaders invest in their people, they create cohesive teams that can follow a known process and accomplish difficult missions.

But accomplishing the mission requires more than talent.

Teams must have an unshakable belief—built through trust, preparation, shared experience, and hardship—that they will succeed.

That belief is one of the most important things a leader can build.
