I have received more than 400 “challenge coins” over 33 years of military service.

I do not remember every training event, deployment, or operation from those years.

But I remember almost every person who handed me one of those coins.

![Shelves of challenge coins](/uploads/blog/2026/coin-tray-1596x1600.jpg)

That is because recognition is rarely about the object itself. It is about observation gained through leader presence. And the memory of leaders who took the time to recognize performance face-to-face.

Strong organizations understand that cohesive teams do not form accidentally.

In the military, we built team cohesion through:

- Shared hardship
- Organizational history
- Common values
- Recognition and reward

Challenge coins became one small but meaningful symbol of those experiences.

Many of the coins in these photos came from leaders I served with during combat operations, disaster relief missions, difficult training events, and organizational crises where teams had to trust one another completely to succeed.

![A display case full of challenge coins](/uploads/blog/2026/coin-table-1600x486.jpg)

There was a very real incentive to perform well because failure carried real consequences.

Over the course of my career, I also gave away thousands of coins to Soldiers, Paratroopers, Rangers, members of the Joint Force, and interagency teammates to honor excellence and recognize contributions to missions for which I bore responsibility.

I learned quickly that recognition matters.

Not because people are looking for trinkets or applause.

A coin handed out casually as a participation trophy carries little meaning.

But recognition tied to observed effort, sacrifice, initiative, or excellence becomes something far more valuable than the object itself.

People know the difference.

Business, academia, and industry use different tools to accomplish the same effect:

- Shared identity
- Public recognition
- Financial incentives
- Advancement opportunities

The tools may be different, but the principle is the same.

People perform better when they feel connected to a team, recognized for contribution, and seen by leaders who are genuinely present.

GEN Stan McChrystal’s Team of Teams work emphasized the importance of trust, shared consciousness, and empowered execution. James MacGregor Burns wrote that transformational leadership elevates motivation and commitment through shared values and purpose.

Both ideas point toward the same truth:

People rarely give their best effort simply because they are paid to do so.

They give their best effort because they feel connected to something larger than themselves—and because leaders take the time to make that connection real.
