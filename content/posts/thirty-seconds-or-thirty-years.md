---
title: "Thirty Seconds or Thirty Years"
date: "2026-12-15"
summary: "Some decisions have to survive thirty seconds. Others have to survive thirty years. The hard part is knowing which tempo the moment needs."
---

**Some decisions have to survive thirty seconds.**

**Others have to survive thirty years.**

I've made both.

One of the hardest things leaders have to get right isn't making the right decision.

It's making the right decision at the right time.

I've worked in environments where waiting meant people died.

I've also worked in environments where acting too quickly made the situation worse.

And I've made decisions whose full effects weren't visible for years.

Those experiences taught me that leadership isn't just about making good decisions.

It's about understanding the environment well enough to know when those decisions are ready to be made.

As a commander in the 3d Ranger Battalion, speed mattered. Information was incomplete, the enemy had a vote, and hesitation often meant surrendering the initiative. Years of realistic, repetitive training allowed leaders to recognize patterns, make rapid decisions, observe the results, adjust immediately, and continue moving. Success came from stringing together enough good decisions to maintain the initiative while correcting imperfect ones before the enemy could exploit them.

At U.S. Central Command, the environment was entirely different. The volume of information was almost overwhelming. Intelligence, operations, diplomacy, cyber, logistics, and partner reporting all competed for attention. Decisions still had to be made, but they depended on systems that distilled enormous amounts of information into decision-quality recommendations.

During Operation Inherent Resolve, I experienced something different again.

The most valuable currency became observation.

Not action.

We worked alongside the U.S. Department of State, coalition partners, Iraqi Security Forces, the Syrian Democratic Forces, and others—each viewing the same environment through a different lens and moving at a different pace.

Sometimes introducing energy into the system too early made the situation worse.

Sometimes waiting created opportunities that action never could.

I also had the privilege of serving under two extraordinary commanders whose decision tempos could not have been more different.

One iterated continuously. Guidance evolved throughout the day as new information emerged. The organization became exceptionally agile because adaptation became part of its rhythm.

The other was remarkably deliberative. He believed the environment still had something to teach him before committing the organization to a consequential decision.

He watched.

He listened.

He allowed ambiguity to mature before acting.

His organization became comfortable operating in uncertainty because waiting was understood as an intentional act of leadership—not an absence of it.

Neither leader was universally right.

Both were exceptionally effective.

**Decision tempo should be informed by the environment—not exclusively by a leader's natural decision style.**

Both understood the environment they were leading—and adjusted their decision tempo accordingly.

Daniel Kahneman reminds us that speed can amplify cognitive bias.

Gary Klein demonstrates that experienced leaders can make remarkably accurate rapid decisions through pattern recognition.

At first glance, those ideas appear contradictory.

They aren't.

They describe different decision environments.

**Some decisions aren't judged in the moment. They're judged years later.**

As Commander of Operations Group at JRTC, I committed resources to a long-term effort to improve training realism in the Northern Training Area. It wasn't a decision that could produce immediate results. It required accepting that the evidence of success might not appear until years after the decision had been made.

When I returned as the Commanding General and flew over the same landscape, the transformation was unmistakable.

The decision had outlived the leader who made it.

And that is precisely why its timing mattered.

Some leadership decisions are measured in minutes.

Others are measured in decades.

Organizations rarely fail because they moved too quickly or too slowly.

They fail because they applied the wrong decision tempo to the environment they faced.

**The best leaders don't simply make good decisions.**

**They know when those decisions are ready to be made.**
