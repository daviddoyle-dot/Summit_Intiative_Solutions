Organizations don't find problems.

They create them.

Not by causing them.

By defining them.

Most leaders are taught to solve problems.

Far fewer are taught that the most important leadership decision often comes before the solution:

What problem are we actually ***deciding*** to solve?

During my time at the School of Advanced Military Studies (SAMS), I was introduced to design theory and the concept of problem framing. The basic idea is simple:

A problem is the gap between the current state and a desired future state.

The challenge is that leaders rarely possess a complete view of either one.

This lesson came to life for me during walking town halls through military family housing.

The purpose was simple: get out from behind reports, dashboards, and briefings and listen directly to residents.

What we discovered was that the situation often "talked back."

We entered some conversations believing we understood the problem.

Then residents showed us something different.

One young girl told me she preferred playing at a friend's neighborhood playground because their slide was smoother and faster. What began as a child's observation led us to discover that aging playground equipment across multiple neighborhoods needed upgrades, prompting us to use existing repair inventories to improve playgrounds throughout the installation. This small comment gave us a new way of looking at the “problem” of building confidence with our residents.

![Talking with a young resident during a walking town hall](/uploads/blog/2026/walking-town-hall-1600x1066.jpg)

Sometimes the person closest to a problem is a child who simply knows which slide is faster.

There were certainly maintenance issues.

But many residents were describing something else:

Communication.

Expectations.

Predictability.

Trust.

Residents observed realities that leaders could not see from briefings and dashboards. Leaders possessed information about resources, constraints, competing priorities, and future plans that residents could not see.

Neither perspective was complete by itself.

We did not simply allow circumstances to define the problem for us.

We synthesized multiple perspectives, identified the future state we wanted to create, and then deliberately framed the problems that needed solving.

In some cases, what appeared to be a maintenance problem was actually a communication problem.

The circumstances were the same.

The problem definition was different.

And different problem definitions lead to different solutions.

One of the most important lessons I learned as a leader is that opportunities often emerge the moment you realize you have defined the wrong problem.
