---
title: "Nothing Was Staged"
date: "2026-12-01"
summary: "Inviting leaders two levels down to shadow me for 48 hours made them better leaders, and made me one too."
---

Successful organizations rely on effective process.

But effective leaders understand that process exists to harness the talent, judgment, and energy of people.

Last week I wrote about the “Three Rings of Organizational Effectiveness” — process maps, operating cadence, and terms of reference. Those systems matter. But systems alone do not build great organizations.

People do.

One of the most valuable leadership tools I used in the later stages of my career was a “shadow program.” I learned how effective the program could be because I had served as a ‘shadow’ earlier in my career. I invited leaders two levels below me—not my direct reports, but subordinate commanders and staff leaders—to spend 48 to 72 hours shadowing me through the full scope of my job.

They sat in meetings, traveled to site visits, joined teleconferences, attended training events, shared meals, and saw the full rhythm of the day.

Nothing was staged.

I wanted them to see the real work: how information arrived, how I made decisions, where friction existed, how priorities changed, and how leaders succeeded—or struggled—in difficult situations. I also shared lessons from my own career—things I had done well, things I would do differently, and hard-earned lessons about balancing family and work, listening with intent, and using automation effectively to support leadership rather than replace it.

Three important things happened every time we ran the program:

1.  **I learned more about my people.**

I learned about their families, their interests, their ambitions, and the unique strengths they brought to the organization. Having two leaders in each session also helped me see how they interacted with peers. Spending extended time together gave me a much better understanding of the talent we had across the team.

2.  **They learned more about the organization.**

They gained context that is difficult to get through emails, memos, or briefings. They saw how senior leaders influence their bosses, receive and process information, how the organization balances its priorities, and how it spends its energy on a routine basis.

3.  **I learned more about the organization itself.**

Because we built trust during those shadow sessions, people were more candid. They pointed out blind spots, surfaced friction points, and asked questions that forced me to think more deeply about issues that mattered to them. I also scrutinized my own use of time more carefully (HT to Jamie Efaw). Every session made me a better leader and made the organization stronger.

This process confirmed to me that investing in people is not separate from mission accomplishment—it is one of the most important ways leaders achieve it. The shadow program required a significant investment of time and energy, especially when leaders were in different locations or served in different roles.

But it was worth it.

Those leaders left better prepared to lead their own teams, more confident in how to execute intent, and more willing to engage openly because trust had been established.

Strong organizations are built through process.

Great organizations are built when leaders invest directly in people.
